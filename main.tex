\begin{abstract}
This paper studies how the elasticity of substitution between labor and AI services shapes
AI research, economic growth, real wages, and factor shares. An integrated monopolist
supplies AI services and invests in better AI. Final output is Cobb--Douglas in capital and
a CES composite of labor and AI services, while AI research combines human researchers and
automated research services through a second CES technology. Human and automated research
inputs are assumed to be gross substitutes, and relative input costs determine the research
mix.

The conditional asymptotic analysis distinguishes three regimes. Below one, any
nondegenerate interior constant-growth candidate satisfying the stated developer
optimality limits has zero per-capita output growth and research inputs grow more slowly
than population. At one, an AI-dominated constant-growth candidate has positive finite rates
only if diminishing returns dominate the feedback from output to automated research and the
consumption--output ratio is positive. Above one, no interior steady state can combine
finite constant growth rates with positive constant AI-capability growth. When the
elasticity is only slightly above one, however, the relative-price
threshold at which this force becomes quantitatively important can be extremely large.
Even when both elasticities equal one, a proportional-spending counterfactual
accelerates if the feedback from output to automated research offsets diminishing
returns; a strict excess produces a finite-time singularity. Thus gross substitution
in final production is not necessary for explosive growth.
An expansion of AI services can lower labor's share while raising the real wage; along an
equilibrium transition, the wage response depends jointly on substitution and capital
deepening. Monopoly restricts current AI deployment but makes frontier improvements
privately valuable through operating rents.
\end{abstract}
