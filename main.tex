Artificial Intelligence, Market Structure, and the Dynamics of Research}
\author{Oliver Pardo}

\begin{abstract}
This paper studies a dynamic economy in which artificial intelligence raises current
productivity and can also be used to produce better artificial intelligence.
Population grows exogenously, competitive final-good firms use quality-adjusted AI
services, and one integrated frontier developer supplies those services as a
monopolist and invests in AI research. Frontier improvements combine human and
automated-research services through a CES technology with research elasticity
$\sigma_{HM}>1$.
The automated share rises when the research wage increases relative to its unit
resource cost, and the human contribution can become asymptotically negligible.
Research automation does not, however, imply that human employment in AI development
falls monotonically. The human-to-automated research ratio $H/M$ can decline while
the human-research employment share $H/N$ rises if the scale of automated research
per capita expands sufficiently quickly.
During a human-dominated phase, capability growth remains semi-endogenous and tied
to population growth. When automated research dominates, capability growth is tied
instead to the growth of automated-research services. In the Cobb--Douglas production
benchmark, a constant-growth path exists only when diminishing returns in ideas
production dominate the feedback between output and automated research. With a
fully Cobb--Douglas research technology, human research remains essential. The paper
maintains the corresponding stability condition
$1-\phi-\eta\omega_M\omega_X/\omega_L>0$ throughout. The AI-dominated condition removes
$\omega_M$ and is therefore stricter. Research automation can consequently eliminate a
stabilizing human bottleneck and move a parameterization that is stable under
Cobb--Douglas research into an acceleration regime. Along the CES transition, a
zero feedback denominator produces acceleration without a finite-time singularity;
a persistent negative denominator produces a singularity under the reduced
proportional-allocation closure. This comparison presumes Cobb--Douglas production. When production labor
and AI services are gross substitutes, $\sigma_{XL}>1$, the output--capital ratio and
the return to capital diverge if capability is unbounded. The household Euler equation
then rules out an interior steady state with finite constant growth rates; human
essentiality in research alone does not restore balanced growth. This break at unit
elasticity is a singular perturbation: finite allocations are continuous in
$\sigma_{XL}$, but the limits $w/p^X\to\infty$ and
$\sigma_{XL}\downarrow1$ do not commute. The scale required for AI dominance
diverges as the elasticity approaches one. A task-based extension makes the set of automated research tasks endogenous
through task-specific fixed costs. With a general CES between production labor and
AI services, the production elasticity
$\sigma_{XL}$ produces different asymptotic growth regimes. The production elasticity
$\sigma_{XL}$ also separates the response of the real wage from that of the
production-labor share. The aggregate labor-income share depends additionally on whether
workers move into or out of human research. In the Cobb--Douglas research
counterfactual, however, a fixed automated-research spending share makes the human
research wage bill a constant share of output. The production and aggregate
labor-income shares then rise together when $\sigma_{XL}<1$ and remain constant when
$\sigma_{XL}=1$. When $\sigma_{XL}>1/\alpha$, wage growth is
positive only when capital deepening offsets the
displacement effect of AI-services deepening. Numerical counterexamples show that a
sufficiently high $\sigma_{XL}$ can make the real wage fall even while capital per
production worker rises. Monopoly restricts current AI deployment but creates the
rents that give frontier capability a private value. Other numerical
experiments illustrate slow convergence in the constant-share benchmark and
acceleration under either strong production substitution or sufficiently strong
research feedback.
\end{abstract}
