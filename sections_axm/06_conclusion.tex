\section{Conclusion}

This paper studies an economy in which current AI capability raises the
productivity of compute used both to supply current AI services and to improve
future AI. When one unit of raw compute costs one, a unit of either
capability-augmented service has resource cost \(1/A\). Placing \(A\) inside
\(AM\) creates a recursive innovation channel: better AI lowers the resource
cost of automated research services and thereby raises future capability.
In the language of \citet{davidsonetal2026}, this is the technological loop. The
economic loop runs from capability to output and from output to accumulation and
research resources. In this paper, their effective strengths depend jointly on
primitive parameters and equilibrium allocations and prices.

Three results organize the analysis. First, conditional on \(Aw\to\infty\), the
research elasticity determines whether the research-expenditure share allocated
to automated services
tends to zero, remains at \(\omega_M\), or tends to one.
Second, the final-production elasticity governs whether human labor remains a
long-run bottleneck. On any finite-rate balanced-growth path satisfying the
conditions characterized under complementarity, output per person is constant
when production labor remains a positive population share. For the interior
candidate with unit elasticity in final production characterized here, positive
capability growth requires
a positive feedback denominator. Its reciprocal is the scalar analogue of
\citet{davidsonetal2026}'s feedback multiplier; this condition alone does not establish
feasibility or existence. With gross substitution in final production, if AI services asymptotically
dominate the labor--AI composite, unbounded capability makes \(Y/K\) and the
return to capital unbounded, ruling out finite constant growth rates.

Third, factor shares and factor prices must be distinguished. Consider the
conditional accelerating branch of the necessary conditions in which AI
services dominate the labor--AI composite. Both labor-income
measures vanish relative to output: \(wL/Y\to0\) and \(wN/Y\to0\). Yet the real
wage eventually rises when the smaller of the final-production and research
elasticities of substitution is below \(1/(\alpha\eta)\) along the characterized
limit; above that threshold the real wage eventually falls. A vanishing share does not by itself imply a
falling level when the scale of the economy grows sufficiently fast.
At the threshold itself, the leading asymptotic term is zero and the model does
not sign the eventual wage change.

The complementarity computations show that automating research does not
necessarily remove labor as an aggregate constraint. With
\(\sigma_{XL}=0.75\), both reported candidate paths approach constant output per
person, consumption per person, and real wages while capability continues to
grow. When \(\sigma_{HM}=2\), the automated research-expenditure share approaches
one and the human-research population share approaches zero, yet aggregate labor
income remains close to its positive analytical limit. These are audited
finite-horizon approximations to a conditional balanced-growth path, not an
existence proof.

The unit-elasticity perfect-foresight simulations likewise illustrate the
analytical results without being used as proofs. At unit elasticity in final production,
both Cobb--Douglas and gross-substitutes research path approximations move toward
the corresponding balanced-growth rates over the reported horizons; they are
constructed using those terminal limits.
In the reported approximation in which automated research services are gross
substitutes for human research, the human-research employment share is hump-shaped:
it can increase for centuries in the numerical normalization before automation
eventually dominates the research-input index and its expenditure share approaches
one. Separating
\(M\) from \(AM\) makes this transition visible. The simulations also display
the Euler-equation link between consumption growth and the net real interest
rate. These paths are numerical illustrations, not forecasts of calendar time.

The free-boundary calculation with gross substitution in both sectors adds a
different numerical result. As the terminal value of \(Y/K\) increases from 16
to 128, the initial jump variables remain unchanged to numerical precision and
the analytically extrapolated singularity date converges. Across these
finite-boundary approximations, growth and the interest rate remain modest for a
long interval and then rise sharply, while the labor-income share approaches zero
and the wage continues to rise. The calculation validates convergence on common
pre-singular intervals and toward successively larger boundaries; it does not
establish that a solution exists all the way to the singularity, establish an
infinite-horizon equilibrium, or turn the normalized time axis into a forecast.
The delayed acceleration resembles the transition emphasized by
\citet{davidsonetal2026}, but their fixed-share calibration and simulated dates
are not transferable to this equilibrium model.

Several extensions are important. A direct term \(A^{1-b}\) could distinguish
intertemporal idea difficulty from the research-scale parameter \(\eta\): holding
the effective-research index fixed, \(b\) governs the factor \(A^{-b}\) in
capability growth, while \(1-b\) governs the stock's separate level effect on
\(\dot A\). A larger extension would follow
\citet{davidsonetal2026} by separating software capability, hardware quality, and
general productivity into distinct stocks with cross-technology feedbacks. Their
matrix notation would organize the technology network; its spectral-radius
classification would apply if the relevant feedback weights converge to constants.
The present Ramsey--monopoly structure would determine prices and allocations.
Physical capital could also enter AI research explicitly, separating
chips, datacenters, and energy infrastructure. Alternative market structures
could clarify how competition changes the balance between current inference
supply and research investment. A further extension is the constant-returns
boundary \(\eta=1\), where the research first-order condition no longer determines
an interior scale.

Finally, greater automation of AI research may create agency and supervision
problems that are absent here. If human oversight is needed to keep automated
research aligned with the developer's objective, an integrated monopolist may
internalize part of that problem and moderate research speed. Competing developers
may instead reduce supervision to avoid losing a race. That extension would make
human research valuable not only as a productive input but also as a governance
input. It could provide a disciplined basis for studying subsidies to human AI
research or taxes on insufficiently supervised research automation; those policy
claims require an explicit agency model and do not follow from the present
technology alone.
