\section{Conclusion}

The paper asks what changes when current AI capability augments not only
inference compute but also compute used to improve AI. The answer is not a
notational detail. When one unit of raw compute costs one, one unit of automated
research service has resource cost \(1/A\). Relative to a specification in which
research uses raw \(M\), placing \(A\) inside \(AM\) adds a capability term to
the developer's costate equation and strengthens the feedback from AI improvement
to future AI improvement.

Three results organize the analysis. First, conditional on \(Aw\to\infty\), the
research elasticity determines whether the research-expenditure share allocated
to automated services
tends to zero, remains at \(\omega_M\), or tends to one.
Second, the final-production elasticity governs whether human labor remains a
long-run bottleneck. On any finite-rate balanced-growth path satisfying the
conditions characterized under complementarity, output per person is constant
when production labor remains a positive population share. For the interior
candidate with unit elasticity in final production characterized here, positive
capability growth requires
a positive feedback denominator; this condition alone does not establish
feasibility or existence. With gross substitution, if AI services asymptotically
dominate the labor--AI composite, unbounded capability makes \(Y/K\) and the
return to capital unbounded, ruling out finite constant growth rates.

Third, factor shares and factor prices must be distinguished. Consider the
conditional accelerating branch of the necessary conditions in which AI
services dominate the labor--AI composite. Both labor-income
measures vanish relative to output: \(wL/Y\to0\) and \(wN/Y\to0\). Yet the real
wage eventually rises when the final-production elasticity of substitution is
below \(1/(\alpha\eta)\) along the characterized limit; above that threshold the
real wage eventually falls. A vanishing share does not by itself imply a
falling level when the scale of the economy grows sufficiently fast.
At the threshold itself, the leading asymptotic term is zero and the model does
not sign the eventual wage change.

The perfect-foresight simulations illustrate the unit-elasticity analytical
results without being used as proofs. At unit elasticity in final production,
both Cobb--Douglas and gross-substitutes research candidate paths converge toward the corresponding
balanced-growth rates.
In the reported candidate path in which automated research services are gross
substitutes for human research, the human-research employment share is hump-shaped:
it can increase for centuries in the numerical normalization before automation
eventually dominates. Separating
\(M\) from \(AM\) makes this transition visible. The simulations also confirm
the Euler-equation link between consumption growth and the net real interest
rate. These paths are numerical illustrations, not forecasts of calendar time.

Several extensions are important. A direct standing-on-shoulders term could be
added to determine how much it amplifies the feedback already carried by \(AM\).
Physical capital could enter AI research explicitly, separating chips,
datacenters, energy infrastructure, and model capability. Alternative market
structures could clarify how competition changes the balance between current
inference supply and research investment. A further extension is the
constant-returns boundary \(\eta=1\), where the research first-order condition no
longer determines an interior scale.

Finally, greater automation of AI research may create agency and supervision
problems that are absent here. If human oversight is needed to keep automated
research aligned with the developer's objective, an integrated monopolist may
internalize part of that problem and moderate research speed. Competing developers
may instead reduce supervision to avoid losing a race. That extension would make
human research valuable not only as a productive input but also as a governance
input. It could provide a disciplined basis for studying subsidies to human AI
research or taxes on insufficiently supervised research automation; those policy
claims require an explicit agency model and do not follow from the present
technology alone.
