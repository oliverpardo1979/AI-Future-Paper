\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The economic consequences of advanced AI depend on more than whether machines can
perform existing jobs. A distinct transition occurs when AI can perform the research
required to produce better AI. The toy model represents this transition as a gradual
change in the composition of research. When human and automated-research services are
gross substitutes ($\sigma_{HM}>1$), the automated share rises with the research wage
relative to its unit resource cost.

Population growth matters while human research dominates. With $\phi<1$ and a
constant research-employment share, capability growth is semi-endogenous and equals
$\eta n/(1-\phi)$. When automated research dominates, capability growth is tied
instead to the growth of automated-research services. In the Cobb--Douglas production
benchmark, a constant-growth path exists if
$1-\phi-\eta\beta/(1-\alpha-\beta)>0$. If this condition fails, the feedback between
output and automated research is too strong for a positive constant-growth solution.

The absence of a constant-growth solution does not by itself establish a
finite-time singularity. Under the paper's reduced proportional-allocation closure,
a positive feedback denominator produces a stable capability-growth rate. A zero
denominator produces exponential growth in the capability growth rate, and therefore
double-exponential capability, but no divergence at a finite date. A persistent
negative denominator produces a finite-time singularity. During the CES transition,
the exact condition depends on the path of
$D(s^M_t)=1-\phi-\eta\upsilon s^M_t$, not only on its AI-dominated limit. These
claims apply to the reduced closure; the fully optimizing equilibrium may reach a
resource constraint or boundary allocation first.

Human essentiality determines which feedback condition applies. If research is
Cobb--Douglas in human and automated inputs, balanced growth instead requires
$1-\phi-\eta\omega_M\omega_X/\omega_L>0$. We impose this restriction throughout
the paper, so the human-essential Cobb--Douglas benchmark is stable by construction.
This condition is weaker because only the automated-input elasticity $\omega_M$
transmits the expansion of output back into research. There is therefore a parameter region in which growth remains balanced
when human research is essential but accelerates when automated research can become
asymptotically dominant. The high-feedback numerical experiment lies in this region.
The Cobb--Douglas comparison is a counterfactual technology, not a change in the
research elasticity during the CES transition.

The task-based extension identifies a separate extensive margin. Even when AI has a
lower marginal cost for a research task, a fixed integration and verification cost
can make human performance cheaper at low scale. Capability growth, a higher research
wage, or an expansion of the research program can move tasks across their automation
thresholds. The resulting aggregate elasticity can vary endogenously with the
distribution of task-level costs, while the CES remains the parsimonious baseline.

The production elasticity $\sigma_{XL}$ also separates the wage response from the
response of the production-labor share. An expansion of AI services reduces this share when
$\sigma_{XL}>1$, but it raises the wage when $\sigma_{XL}<1/\alpha$. Hence both outcomes occur
when $1<\sigma_{XL}<1/\alpha$. This conditional result is a benchmark, not the paper's
standalone contribution. In general equilibrium, capital deepening and the
reallocation of workers between production and research also affect wages and the
aggregate labor share. More precisely, aggregate labor-share growth equals
production labor-share growth plus the growth of population relative to production
labor. A rising human-research employment share raises the aggregate share relative
to the production share; a declining human-research share lowers it.

Research automation also has two distinct margins within the AI-development
industry. The ratio $H/M$ measures human intensity, while $H/N$ measures the scale
of human research employment. The first can fall while the second rises. This occurs
when automated research per capita expands more rapidly than human input per unit of
automated research contracts. The numerical experiments produce both a monotone
decline in $H/N$ and a U-shaped path in which human research employment first falls
and then expands, even though $H/M$ declines throughout. The asymptotic disappearance
of human research as a relative input therefore does not imply that human research
employment must fall during the transition.

The production elasticity determines whether the economy reaches that asymptotic
research regime. When $\sigma_{XL}<1$, labor remains essential and the productive return
to further reductions in the unit cost of AI services vanishes. When $\sigma_{XL}=1$,
positive wage growth makes automated research asymptotically dominant. When
$\sigma_{XL}>1$ and the effective unit cost of AI services vanishes relative to
capital intensity, the production-labor share converges to zero. More generally, if
capability is unbounded, the return to capital diverges. The household Euler
equation then makes consumption growth diverge, so no interior steady state with
finite constant growth rates exists. The qualification is important:
$\sigma_{XL}>1$ alone does not exclude a boundary path with bounded capability.

The discontinuity at unit elasticity is an infinite-horizon classification, not a
jump in finite allocations. At every finite relative price, the CES contribution
share is continuous in $\sigma_{XL}$. But the limits $w/p^X\to\infty$ and
$\sigma_{XL}\downarrow1$ do not commute: the share remains $\omega_X$ at exactly
one and converges to one for every fixed elasticity above one. Under the paper's
high-capability regularity condition, unbounded capability induces this relative-price
limit. The relative-price threshold for the
transition diverges as $\sigma_{XL}$ approaches one. This makes the result a singular
perturbation and explains why an economy with $\sigma_{XL}$ only slightly above one
can look Cobb--Douglas for a very long time without possessing a finite-growth
steady state asymptotically.

The numerical experiments show why transitional dynamics cannot be inferred from
these asymptotic results alone. In the stable Cobb--Douglas benchmark, research
automation is sufficiently gradual that the economy remains below its
AI-dominated growth rate for centuries. Different initial capability stocks produce
different early growth rates but converge to the same path. With $\sigma_{XL}=2$,
however, output- and consumption-per-capita growth initially fall and then turn
upward. The path reaches the numerical cutoff in year 196, when their growth rates
are approximately 71 percent per year. The reacceleration therefore does not require
the earlier $\sigma_{XL}=4$ extreme. The near-unit experiments show the other side
of the same result. The $\sigma_{XL}=1.01$ path remains close to Cobb--Douglas after
one thousand simulated years, while $\sigma_{XL}=1.05$ shows visible but still
moderate reacceleration and $\sigma_{XL}=1.10$ reaches the cutoff in year 744. The
simulations therefore reject both an immediate-takeoff reading of
$\sigma_{XL}>1$ and an inference of eventual balanced growth from a long,
apparently stable transition. The wage experiments
also reject an unconditional wage-growth result. Wage growth equals a
capital-deepening term plus an AI-services-deepening term whose sign turns negative
when $\sigma_{XL}>1/\alpha$. Under high population growth and a very high production
elasticity $\sigma_{XL}$, the negative latter term can dominate even when the capital
term is positive. The labor-share experiments also show that the production and aggregate shares need not
move together. In the baseline with $\sigma_{HM}=2$ and $\sigma_{XL}=1$, the
production share remains constant while the aggregate share falls as the
human-research employment share declines. Under Cobb--Douglas research,
$\sigma_{HM}=1$, the result changes. A fixed automated-research spending share makes
the human-research wage bill a constant share of output. The production and
aggregate labor-income shares then rise by the same number of percentage points
when $\sigma_{XL}<1$ and both remain constant when $\sigma_{XL}=1$. In the
complementarity experiments, this distributional protection coincides with
per-capita output growth converging to zero. More generally, any candidate interior
constant-growth limit with $\sigma_{XL}<1$ has $g_Y=n$ and
$g_A=\eta n/(1-\phi)$. By contrast, $\sigma_{XL}>1$ rules out an interior
steady state whenever $A_t\to\infty$, while a negative value of
$1-\phi-\eta\omega_X/\omega_L$ generates an additional research-feedback
acceleration mechanism under Cobb--Douglas production. These experiments use fixed investment and
research-spending shares; a full quantitative equilibrium must endogenize both
decisions before drawing welfare or policy conclusions.

The return to capital supplies the price counterpart to these quantity dynamics.
The net marginal return is $r_t-\delta=\alpha Y_t/K_t-\delta$. It moves toward four
percent in the complementarity experiment and remains close to that value in the
Cobb--Douglas experiment, but eventually rises under gross substitution as output
expands faster than capital. With $\sigma_{XL}=2$, the net return first falls to 4.39
percent and then rises to 78.45 percent before the numerical cutoff. Because the
simulations fix the investment share, these paths are marginal
returns implied by production rather than intertemporal prices from the fully
optimizing equilibrium. Endogenizing saving is necessary to determine how strongly
capital accumulation attenuates the increase in the interest rate.

Taken together, these results explain why the automation of AI research matters for
humans even when the real wage does not fall. It can change the growth regime, reduce
labor's share of an expanding economy, increase the return to capital, and shift
income toward the owners of AI firms and other productive assets. It can also reduce
the number of humans required per unit of research without reducing human research
employment, because the development industry may expand faster than its human
intensity falls. The time dimension is equally important: an economy can display
moderate growth for a long period and still lack an asymptotic finite-growth steady
state. Observed stability over a finite transition therefore does not by itself
identify the long-run regime.

The current model does not establish whether an acceleration regime improves human
welfare. Monopoly rents accrue to a representative household, so the model abstracts
from unequal ownership and understates the distributional importance of a declining
labor share. It also excludes agency failures, misalignment, existential damage, and
political power. The numerical closure fixes investment and automated-research
spending shares and should be read as a collection of mechanism experiments rather
than as a quantitative forecast or a solved optimal transition. The paper's result
is therefore conditional: it identifies the economic mechanisms and parameter
regions that produce balanced growth, acceleration, or singularity, but it does not
assign probabilities to those regimes or determine their full welfare ranking.

Market power creates a genuine policy trade-off. The monopolist restricts the current
supply of AI services, but its operating rents raise the private return to frontier
research. Because it does not appropriate all consumer surplus and knowledge
benefits, private research remains insufficient whenever $Q_t>q_t$. Regulating the
AI-service price without replacing the lost innovation return can therefore improve
current deployment while slowing capability growth. The natural policy comparison is
between laissez-faire monopoly, regulated access, explicit research support, and a
joint policy that targets both margins.

A further extension should make explicit an agency problem that is only implicit in
the current model. As $H/M$ falls and automated systems choose a larger share of the
experiments, code, and model modifications that move the frontier, human owners and
researchers may become less able to observe their actions, verify their objectives,
or anticipate the consequences of every research decision. Supervision would then
be a costly choice rather than an implicit component of $H_t$. More intensive
oversight could reduce the probability that automated research departs from the
human principal's objectives, but it could also slow capability growth. Research
automation would thus generate a principal--agent problem with costly monitoring in
the spirit of \citet{holmstrom1979}.

Market structure may alter this trade-off, although its sign is not predetermined.
A protected monopolist internalizes a larger share of the loss to its installed
rents and franchise value if its automated researchers escape effective control. It
also faces less pressure to preempt a competitor. These forces may induce it to
moderate capability growth or invest more in supervision. Under monopolistic
competition, by contrast, each developer captures the private benefit of arriving
first, while part of the expected cost of inadequate supervision is borne by rivals
and society. Firms may consequently underinvest in oversight in order to accelerate
development and preserve their competitive position. Monopoly is not, however, a
guarantee of better control: concentration creates its own governance problems, and
the threat of entry or a foreign rival can restore the race incentive. The race and
slowdown branches in \citet{kokotajlo2025} illustrate precisely this tension: even a
concentrated frontier developer that observes evidence of misalignment may hesitate
to pause when another developer is close behind. A formal extension should therefore
allow developers to choose innovation speed and supervisory effort, make the
probability of an agency failure depend on automation and oversight, and compare
monopoly with monopolistic competition. If human research provides supervision that
is socially valuable but not fully remunerated, the extension could also evaluate a
second-best policy that subsidizes $H_t$ and taxes $M_t$. The purpose of these wedges
would not be to preserve human research employment, but to correct the effect of the
research-input mix on agency risk. A subsidy to verifiable supervision or liability
for agency failures would generally target the distortion more directly. A subsidy
to $H_t$ and a tax on $M_t$ become relevant when supervision cannot be separately
observed or contracted. These policies cannot be evaluated in the current model,
which contains neither agency losses nor an explicit supervision technology. The
central open question is when the
monopolist's internalization of agency losses dominates the governance costs of
concentration, and when competition turns effective supervision into an externality.
