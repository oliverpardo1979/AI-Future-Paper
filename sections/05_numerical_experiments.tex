\section{Numerical transition experiments}
\label{sec:numerical}

This section uses numerical experiments to distinguish transitional dynamics from
asymptotic growth. The exercises are illustrative rather than a quantitative
calibration. They preserve the production CES, monopoly service supply, the
cost-minimizing CES research mix, population growth, capital accumulation, and the
ideas production function. They do not yet solve the full saddle path for household
consumption and the developer's shadow value of capability.

\subsection{Numerical closure and baseline}

To close the transition system transparently, investment and automated-research
spending are fixed at constant shares of output:
\begin{equation}
    I_t=s_IY_t,
    \qquad
    \xi M_t=m_RY_t.
    \label{eq:numerical-share-rules}
\end{equation}
Conditional on $(K_t,A_t,N_t)$, the algorithm jointly solves the monopoly condition
\eqref{eq:monopoly-ai-price}, labor-market clearing, and the research-input ratio
\eqref{eq:research-input-ratio}. The state variables then follow
\begin{align}
    \dot K_t&=s_IY_t-\delta K_t,
    &
    \dot A_t&=\chi A_t^\phi E_t^\eta,
    &
    \dot N_t&=nN_t.
    \label{eq:numerical-state-system}
\end{align}
Consumption is the residual
\begin{equation}
    \frac{C_t}{Y_t}
    =
    1-s_I-m_R-\frac{\xi U_t}{Y_t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-consumption}
\end{equation}
The closure in \eqref{eq:numerical-share-rules} isolates the technological and
market-structure feedbacks. It should not be interpreted as the developer's optimal
research policy away from the balanced-growth path.

Table~\ref{tab:numerical-calibration} reports the baseline. The investment and
research-compute shares are obtained from
\eqref{eq:bg-research-resource-share} and
\eqref{eq:bg-investment-share}. In the Cobb--Douglas benchmark they imply an
AI-dominated capability growth rate of 2.27 percent and per-capita growth of 0.57
percent. The scale parameter $\chi$ is normalized so that initial capability growth
equals 2.27 percent. In comparisons that change a central elasticity, $\chi$ is
renormalized to retain the same initial growth rate. This makes the subsequent
divergence a dynamic result rather than a difference imposed at date zero.

Every numerical experiment satisfies
Assumption~\ref{ass:stable-human-essential-benchmark}. The baseline has
$D_{\mathrm{CD}}=0.3106$. The high-feedback experiment below changes $\phi$ and
$\eta$ but retains $D_{\mathrm{CD}}=0.08575>0$, even though its AI-dominated
denominator is negative.

The core experiments hold $\alpha$, $n$, $\delta$, and $\rho$ fixed at the values
in Table~\ref{tab:numerical-calibration}. We do not report a generic sensitivity
analysis for these four parameters. The deliberate exception is the wage-decline
exercise below, which raises population growth to construct the labor-supply and
capital-dilution forces in equation~\eqref{eq:numerical-wage-decline-condition}.
That change is a mechanism experiment, not an alternative demographic calibration.

The initial stocks are $A_0=N_0=1$ and $K_0=2.509$. The common compute cost is
normalized to $\xi=1$. All reported terminal growth rates are averages over the
last ten simulated years. The static monopoly and research-mix residuals are below
$10^{-6}$, and the consumption share remains positive in every experiment.

\subsection{Production substitution}

The central comparison changes only the production elasticity $\sigma_{XL}$ between
production labor and AI services, apart from the normalization of $\chi$ described
above. The values $\sigma_{XL}=0.75$ and $\sigma_{XL}=1$ represent complementarity
and the Cobb--Douglas benchmark. The value $\sigma_{XL}=2$ is close to, but above,
the software--labor elasticity of 1.6 estimated by \citet{aumshin2024}. That estimate
does not identify the elasticity between AI services and labor in this model.

The figures deliberately exclude the earlier $\sigma_{XL}=4$ mechanism experiment.
The value $\sigma_{XL}=2$ is sufficient to generate reacceleration, so the main
result does not depend on an extreme substitution elasticity. The complementary and
Cobb--Douglas paths are shown through year 200. The $\sigma_{XL}=2$ path ends in year
196, when capital growth first crosses the numerical cutoff of 50 percent per year.
Population is not plotted because its exogenous path is common across experiments.
The fixed investment and automated-research spending shares are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:numerical-calibration} rather than repeated as constant lines.

The growth rates in Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-growth-rates} reveal a U-shaped
transition when $\sigma_{XL}=2$. Output-per-capita growth falls to 0.38 percent in

11.55 percent at the endpoint. Thus the apparent moderation during the first several
decades is not convergence to balanced growth. Production substitution first raises
the scale of the economy, and the larger scale subsequently finances enough
automated research to reverse the decline in capability growth.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-input-allocation} identifies the quantities behind the

even though the scale of the research sector expands. The real wage also rises

while the level of the wage continues to increase.

large values should not be read as calibrated forecasts. They show why a path that
looks temporarily stable can leave that neighborhood once substitution reallocates
production and research toward machine inputs.

\subsubsection{The neighborhood of unit elasticity}

Proposition~\ref{prop:production-unit-elasticity-knife-edge} shows that the
asymptotic classification is discontinuous at $\sigma_{XL}=1$ even though every
finite allocation is continuous in the elasticity. Figure~\ref{fig:production-knife-edge}
first illustrates the exact CES mapping, independently of the dynamic closure.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/production_knife_edge.png}
    \caption{The unit-elasticity singular perturbation. The left panel uses the
    exact share equation \eqref{eq:production-share-odds}. The right panel reports
    the base-10 logarithm of the relative-price threshold in
    \eqref{eq:production-share-threshold}. The CES weights are the benchmark values
    $\omega_X=0.2$ and $\omega_L=0.8$.}
    \label{fig:production-knife-edge}
\end{figure}

At $\sigma_{XL}=1$, the AI contribution remains at 20 percent for every relative
price. For any fixed elasticity above one it eventually approaches one, but the
relative-price scale required for this transition diverges as the elasticity
approaches one. An AI contribution of 50 percent requires $w/p^X$ of approximately
$10^{60.81}$, $10^{12.64}$, and $10^{6.62}$ when $\sigma_{XL}$ equals 1.01, 1.05,
and 1.10, respectively. These are thresholds in a relative price, not predictions
about calendar time. Their role is to expose the nonuniformity of the asymptotic
limit.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-near-unit-production} feeds the same elasticities into the
dynamic numerical closure. Each case has the benchmark parameters, initial stocks,
spending shares, and initial capability growth. The horizon is one thousand years;
the $\sigma_{XL}=1.10$ path ends earlier when capital growth crosses the numerical
cutoff.

The $\sigma_{XL}=1.01$ economy remains close to the Cobb--Douglas path even at year
1,000: its AI contribution is 22.77 percent, capability growth is 1.95 percent,
output-per-capita growth is 0.58 percent, and the net return is 4.58 percent. The
$\sigma_{XL}=1.05$ path has not reached the cutoff by that date, but reacceleration
is already visible: the corresponding values are 46.23 percent, 4.72 percent, 4.04
percent, and 8.98 percent. With $\sigma_{XL}=1.10$, the transition is substantially
faster. The path reaches the cutoff in year 744, when the AI contribution is 80.99
percent, capability growth is 47.12 percent, output-per-capita growth is 86.08
percent, and the net return is 118.04 percent.

The exercise reconciles the two theoretical results. There is no interior
finite-growth steady state with unbounded capability for any fixed
$\sigma_{XL}>1$, but the time and relative-price scale at which that asymptotic
force becomes quantitatively important can diverge as $\sigma_{XL}\downarrow1$.
Near-unit elasticities can therefore generate a long quasi-Cobb--Douglas transition
without restoring balanced growth in the infinite-horizon limit. The numerical
paths are mechanism experiments, not forecasts over a literal millennium.

\subsection{The return to capital and the numerical closure}

Competitive final-good firms equate the gross rental rate of capital to its marginal
product. The gross and net returns are therefore
\begin{equation}
    r_t=\alpha\frac{Y_t}{K_t},
    \qquad
    r_t-\delta=\alpha\frac{Y_t}{K_t}-\delta.
    \label{eq:numerical-capital-return}
\end{equation}
The second object is the net real return that enters the household's Euler equation.
Its dynamics can be read directly from
\begin{equation}
    g_{r,t}=g_{Y,t}-g_{K,t},
    \qquad
    g_{K,t}=\frac{s_I}{\alpha}r_t-\delta.
    \label{eq:numerical-capital-return-growth}
\end{equation}
The rental rate is constant only when output and capital grow at the same rate. If
output grows faster than capital, the capital--output ratio falls and the return to
capital rises.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-interest-rates} shows a non-monotone transition. With
$\sigma_{XL}=0.75$, the net return starts at 2.17 percent, reaches 4.34 percent in
year 71, and equals 3.95 percent in year 200. In the Cobb--Douglas case it moves
from 4.57 to 4.33 percent over the same horizon. These values do not describe two
different calibrations of the initial interest rate. The experiments hold the
initial stocks fixed, so the change in $\sigma_{XL}$ changes initial output and the
marginal product of capital.

The dynamics reverse under stronger substitution. With $\sigma_{XL}=2$, the net
return falls from 8.25 to 4.39 percent by year 65, but subsequently rises to 8.73
percent in year 160 and 78.45 percent at the year-196 stopping point. The late
increase is the price counterpart of the acceleration regime: AI raises output
faster than the fixed investment rule accumulates capital, so capital becomes scarce
relative to the effective production input. This reversal occurs without the
$\sigma_{XL}=4$ path used in earlier mechanism experiments.

The lower-right panel reports an important limitation. The numerical closure fixes
$s_I$ and calculates consumption as a residual. It therefore does not impose
$g_{c,t}=r_t-\delta-\rho$ along the transition. A nonzero Euler gap is not a numerical
error; it measures the difference between the fixed-share transition and the
household's optimal saving path. The fully optimizing equilibrium must endogenize
investment. Higher returns would induce additional saving, which could attenuate the
increase in $r_t-\delta$ and change the speed of the transition. At the
$\sigma_{XL}=2$ stopping point, the Euler gap is 4.32 percentage points, so the
terminal fixed-share path is visibly not an optimizing household equilibrium.

\subsection{When does labor's income share rise or fall?}

Proposition~\ref{prop:dynamic-labor-share} separates the income share paid to
production workers from the aggregate labor-income share, which also includes human
researchers. The production-labor share satisfies
\eqref{eq:dynamic-production-labor-share}. It rises when the substitution term
\[
    \left(1-\frac{1}{\sigma_{XL}}\right)
    \left(g_X-g_L\right)
\]
is negative and falls when it is positive. Thus, if AI services per production
worker increase, the threshold is $\sigma_{XL}=1$: complementarity raises the share,
Cobb--Douglas production keeps it constant, and gross substitution lowers it. This
condition does not depend directly on capital accumulation.

The aggregate labor-income share adds the reallocation term
$\dot h/(1-h)=n-g_L$, where $h=H/N$ is the human-research share of population.
An increase in $h$ raises the aggregate share relative to the production-labor
share; a decrease in $h$ lowers it. The aggregate share therefore rises if
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dot h_t}{1-h_t}
    >
    \left(1-\frac{1}{\sigma_{XL}}\right)s^X_t
    \left(g_{X,t}-g_{L,t}\right),
    \label{eq:numerical-aggregate-share-rise}
\end{equation}
and falls if the inequality is reversed. When the research-employment share is
constant, the reallocation term is zero and the $\sigma_{XL}=1$ threshold applies to
both labor-income shares.

\begin{sloppypar}
Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-labor-shares} shows that the distinction is quantitatively
relevant. With $\sigma_{XL}=0.75$, the production-labor share rises from 36.3 to 43.8
percent of output. The aggregate share rises from 43.7 to 47.7 percent. Its increase
is smaller because the human-research share of population falls from 17.0 to 8.1
percent. In the Cobb--Douglas case, the production-labor share remains exactly equal
to 53.6 percent, but the aggregate share falls from 57.8 to 55.3 percent because the
human-research share falls from 7.3 to 3.1 percent.
\end{sloppypar}

With $\sigma_{XL}=2$, the production-labor share falls from 65.6 to 0.72 percent and
the aggregate share falls from 68.1 to 0.79 percent by year 196. Human research rises
from 3.8 to 8.8 percent of the population, so reallocation toward AI development
slightly attenuates the fall in the aggregate share. It does not offset the
displacement term. The comparison with the rising wage in Figure
\ref{fig:numerical-input-allocation} is important: labor can receive a rapidly
growing real wage and a vanishing share of an even more rapidly growing economy.

\subsection{When can the real wage fall?}

The static threshold $\sigma_{XL}=1/\alpha$ is not, by itself, a prediction for the
equilibrium wage path because capital, AI services, and production labor all move.
Define capital and AI-services deepening relative to production labor as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{K,t}\equiv g_{K,t}-g_{L,t},
    \qquad
    \Delta_{X,t}\equiv g_{X,t}-g_{L,t}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dynamic-wage-decomposition} implies the exact condition
\begin{equation}
    g_{w,t}
    =
    \alpha\Delta_{K,t}
    +
    s^X_t\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{XL}}-\alpha\right)\Delta_{X,t}
    <0.
    \label{eq:numerical-wage-decline-condition}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $\sigma_{XL}\leq 1/\alpha$ and both forms of deepening are weakly
positive, the wage cannot fall. This is a sufficient condition, not an unconditional
theorem: the wage can still fall if capital per production worker contracts. When
$\sigma_{XL}>1/\alpha$ and $\Delta_{X,t}>0$, the relevant condition becomes
\begin{equation}
    \alpha\Delta_{K,t}
    <
    s^X_t\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{\sigma_{XL}}\right)\Delta_{X,t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-wage-decline-threshold}
\end{equation}
Thus high substitutability is not sufficient either. AI services must grow rapidly
relative to production labor, their expenditure share must be quantitatively
important, and capital deepening must be insufficient to offset the displacement
term.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-wage-paths} compares four 80-year transitions. The
reference path uses $n=1.2$ percent, $\sigma_{XL}=4$, and the baseline investment share
$s_I=23.34$ percent. The second raises population growth to 3 percent. We do not use
larger values because the household problem requires $\rho>n$ and the calibration
sets $\rho=4$ percent. Neither path falls below its initial wage. In the high
population case, the wage is 17.0 percent above its initial level in year 80.

\begin{remark}[Numerical counterexample: declining real wages]
\label{rem:numerical-wage-counterexample}
Set $n=3$ percent, $\sigma_{XL}=10$, and $s_I=23.34$ percent. Along the resulting
80-year transition, the real wage first rises and reaches a level 12.9 percent above
its initial value in year 50. It subsequently falls, crosses below its initial level
in year 77, and ends 4.3 percent below that level. In year 80, capital deepening
contributes 3.80 percentage points to wage growth, while AI-services deepening
contributes $-5.26$ percentage points. The net wage growth rate is $-1.50$ percent
even though capital per production worker continues to increase. If the investment
share is reduced to 15 percent, the real wage falls from the beginning and ends 18.2
percent below its initial level.

This counterexample establishes that the model does not imply non-decreasing real
wages. It does not establish that a wage decline is quantitatively likely: the
experiment uses a very high production elasticity and a fixed investment share
rather than the household's optimal saving rule.
\end{remark}

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-wage-frontier} broadens the comparison. Each reported
number is the lowest percentage change relative to the initial wage over 80 years.
A zero therefore means that the wage never crosses below its initial value; it does
not rule out a decline from an intervening peak. At $\sigma_{XL}=4$ and $n=3$ percent,
an investment share of 15 percent produces a cumulative loss of 9 percent, whereas
shares of 20 percent or more prevent a loss relative to date zero over the simulated
horizon. At $n=3$ percent and $s_I=20$ percent, the wage remains above its initial
level through $\sigma_{XL}=6$, but falls 4.7 percent below it at $\sigma_{XL}=8$ and 9.1
percent below it at $\sigma_{XL}=10$. Even with the baseline investment share, the
$\sigma_{XL}=10$ case ends 4.3 percent below the initial wage.

    \caption{Numerical frontier for cumulative real-wage losses over 80 years.
    The left panel fixes $\sigma_{XL}=4$; the right panel fixes population growth at

These experiments answer the qualitative question but not yet the quantitative one.
There is no theorem that wages always rise in the current model. There is instead a
sufficient non-decline condition and an endogenous boundary summarized by
\eqref{eq:numerical-wage-decline-condition}. In this numerical closure, population
growth pushes toward wage decline by increasing labor supply and diluting capital,
while investment pushes in the opposite direction. Substitution changes the sign
and magnitude of the AI-services term. Because $s_I$ is fixed in these experiments,
the exact numerical boundary is provisional. An optimizing household may respond to
the prospect of lower wages and higher capital returns by changing saving, which
will shift the frontier.

\subsection{The speed of research automation}

\begin{sloppypar}
Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-research-automation} changes the research elasticity
$\sigma_{HM}$ between $H$ and $M$ while holding the
initial automated-research share at 10.9 percent and initial capability growth at
2.27 percent. The CES weight $\omega_M$ (and hence $\omega_H=1-\omega_M$) and the
scale parameter $\chi$ are renormalized
for this comparison. The exercise therefore isolates the curvature of substitution
rather than confounding it with a different initial input mix.
We are not aware of a direct estimate of $\sigma_{HM}$. Existing work
measures task exposure and complementary bottlenecks rather than substitution
between human and automated inputs inside AI research
\citep{jones2025rd,trammellkorinek2023}.
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_research_automation.png}
    \caption{Research-automation transitions under Cobb--Douglas production.
    The initial automated-research share and capability growth rate are common
    across cases.}

After 400 years, automated services account for 16.0 percent of effective research
when $\sigma_{HM}=1.25$, 38.3 percent when $\sigma_{HM}=2$, and 90.4 percent when
$\sigma_{HM}=4$. The corresponding human-research shares of population are 4.77, 1.51,
and 0.10 percent. A larger elasticity therefore changes the input composition much
more quickly. It does not imply an immediate increase in capability growth.
Capability growth initially falls in all three cases and, with $\sigma_{HM}=4$, begins
to recover only after automated research has become quantitatively important. The
automation share is thus a state of the transition, not a sufficient statistic for
the growth rate.

\subsection{Initial human dominance and the singularity threshold}

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-automation-feedback} solves the internally consistent
reduced system in equations \eqref{eq:ces-transition-growth-dynamics} and
\eqref{eq:reduced-consistent-automation-dynamics}. The exercise holds
$\sigma_{HM}=2$, $(\omega_H,\omega_M)=(0.65,0.35)$, and initial capability growth
at 2.27 percent. It varies
the initial automated share between 0.1, 1, and 10 percent. Equation
\eqref{eq:initial-automation-odds} implies corresponding initial values of
$w_0/\xi_0$ equal to 0.0035, 0.0348, and 0.3832. A small automated share is thus
the equilibrium counterpart of initially inexpensive human research relative to a
standardized unit of automated research. The automated share reaches one half in
years 1,090, 743, and 388.

    the input-growth rates; it is not imposed as an independent path. The last panel
    applies the reduced proportional-allocation closure; each line ends at its
    numerical singularity. The upper-left panel is shown only through
    $w_t/\xi_t=10^6$. The exercise is a mechanism illustration, not a forecast.}
    \label{fig:numerical-automation-feedback}
\end{figure}

In the high-feedback calibration, the critical share in
\eqref{eq:critical-automation-share} is $s^\dagger=90.3$ percent. The feedback
denominator crosses zero in years 1,254, 907, and 560 as the initial automated share
increases. Applying the integral condition in
\eqref{eq:time-varying-singularity-condition}, the reduced system reaches a
finite-time singularity in years 1,452, 1,105, and 759. A lower initial value of
$w_0/\xi_0$ delays the entire transition, but it does not prevent the singularity in
this calibration because $D_{\mathrm{AI}}<0$ and $s^M_t$ eventually converges to
one.

The exercise clarifies why $D_{\mathrm{AI}}<0$ is not an event date. The economy
first has to move from a small automated share to the critical share, and the
negative feedback denominator must then persist long enough for the integral in
\eqref{eq:time-varying-singularity-condition} to reach zero. A rising compute cost,
an endogenous reduction in research spending, or a boundary allocation can delay or
prevent the singularity in the full model.

\subsection{Automation intensity and the scale of AI research}

The decline of human research relative to automated research does not determine
whether the development industry employs more or fewer people. Equation
\eqref{eq:human-research-scale-identity} gives the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}
    \frac{H_t}{N_t}
    =
    \frac{H_t}{M_t}\frac{M_t}{N_t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-research-employment-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first ratio measures the human intensity of research. The second measures the
scale of automated research relative to the population. Under the numerical rule
$\xi M_t=m_RY_t$ and the normalization $g_\xi=0$, their growth rates imply
\begin{equation}
    g_{H/N,t}
    =
    g_{Y,t}-n-\sigma_{HM} g_{w,t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-research-employment-growth}
\end{equation}
Human research employment therefore rises when the expansion of output per capita,
and hence of automated-research spending per capita, exceeds the reduction in human
intensity induced by wage growth.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-research-labor-dynamics} compares $\sigma_{XL}=1$, $2$, and
$2.25$, holding $\sigma_{HM}=2$, the initial stocks, the spending rules, and initial
capability growth fixed. The cases begin with different research-input ratios because
the production elasticity changes the date-zero wage. This is an equilibrium level
effect rather than a different initial state.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_research_labor_dynamics.png}
    \caption{Human employment during AI research automation. The first two panels
    report the human-to-automated research ratio and automated research per capita on
    logarithmic scales. The third reports human researchers as a percentage of the
    population, and the fourth reports the growth rate of that share. The
    $\sigma_{XL}=2.25$ path ends when capital growth crosses 50 percent per year.}
    \label{fig:numerical-research-labor-dynamics}
\end{figure}

With $\sigma_{XL}=1$, $H/M$ falls from 19.51 to 3.78 over 180 years and $H/N$ falls
monotonically from 7.25 to 3.33 percent. With $\sigma_{XL}=2$, $H/M$ falls more sharply,
from 7.32 to 0.29. Nevertheless, $H/N$ reaches a minimum of approximately 3.20
percent around year 65 and then rises to 4.77 percent in year 180. With
$\sigma_{XL}=2.25$, $H/M$ falls from 6.96 to 0.018, while $H/N$ falls initially from
3.65 to approximately 3.11 percent and then rises to 19.46 percent before the path
reaches the acceleration cutoff in year 178.

The last path is not a prediction that one fifth of the population will work in AI
research. It identifies a general-equilibrium mechanism. Rapid output growth expands
$M/N$ from 0.0052 to 10.84, which more than offsets the decline in human researchers
required per unit of automated research. The development industry becomes less human
intensive and employs a larger fraction of the population at the same time. Because
the numerical closure fixes $m_R$, an optimizing developer may move the turning point
or eliminate it. The experiment establishes that $H/M\to0$ is not sufficient for
$H/N\to0$.

\subsection{Initial conditions and asymptotic growth}

Steady-state language requires care. Parameters determine a unique stable
balanced-growth path in the baseline numerical system. Initial conditions determine
the transition toward that path; they determine the asymptotic outcome only if the
model has multiple attractors, thresholds, or an explosive region.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-initial-conditions} holds all parameters fixed and sets
$A_0$ equal to 0.5, 1, or 2. Initial capital and population are common. The output
series use the output level in the $A_0=1$ economy at date zero as a common index,
so convergence in the figure is convergence in levels rather than an artifact of
separate normalization.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/numerical_initial_conditions.png}
    \caption{Transitions from different initial capability stocks in the stable
    Cobb--Douglas parameterization.}
    \label{fig:numerical-initial-conditions}
\end{figure}

The initial capability stock has a large effect on early capability growth: it is
3.0 percent when $A_0=0.5$ and 1.7 percent when $A_0=2$. By year 800, however, the
three capability growth rates lie between 1.64 and 1.65 percent, and the output
levels differ by less than 0.4 percent. Initial conditions mainly shift the
transition. The common growth rate is still below the analytical limit of 2.27
percent because automated services account for only about 73.6 percent of effective
research even after eight centuries. This slow convergence makes it useful to call
the observed finite-horizon path a \emph{transitional growth regime}, not a
``semi-steady state.''

\subsection{Research feedback and acceleration}

The final experiment holds $\sigma_{XL}=1$ and compares the stable baseline,
$1-\phi-\eta\upsilon=0.2375$, with a high-feedback case
$(\phi,\eta)=(0.86,0.62)$, for which
$1-\phi-\eta\upsilon=-0.015$. Initial capability growth and the allocation shares
are again common.

The high-feedback case isolates the role of human essentiality. If research were
Cobb--Douglas, its feedback denominator would be
$1-\phi-\eta\omega_M\omega_X/\omega_L=0.08575>0$. The same values of $\phi$, $\eta$,
$\omega_X$, and $\omega_L$ would then admit a constant-growth path because only the automated-input
elasticity $\omega_M$ transmits the expansion of output back into research. The negative
AI-dominated denominator arises when automated research can asymptotically replace
the human input.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_growth_feedback.png}
    \caption{Stable and accelerating research-feedback regimes under
    Cobb--Douglas production.}
    \label{fig:numerical-growth-feedback}
\end{figure}

In the stable case, capability growth is 1.50 percent in year 600 and automated
services account for 56.2 percent of effective research. In the high-feedback case,
the automated share is 99.6 percent, capability growth reaches 18.3 percent, and
per-capita output growth reaches 4.32 percent. The latter path does not approach a
positive constant growth rate. It illustrates why failure of
\eqref{eq:cd-ai-bg-condition} should be interpreted as a candidate acceleration
regime rather than as another balanced-growth path. It also provides the numerical
counterpart to Proposition~\ref{prop:human-essentiality-acceleration}: the
Cobb--Douglas research counterfactual remains stable, whereas the AI-dominated path
accelerates.

\subsection{Human-essential research with production substitution}

The preceding comparison holds production fixed at $\sigma_{XL}=1$. Figure
\ref{fig:numerical-sigma-hm-one-sigma-xl} instead fixes $\sigma_{HM}=1$, so research is
Cobb--Douglas in $H$ and $M$, and varies the production elasticity. The automated
contribution to effective research is therefore exactly $\omega_M=35$ percent in every
period and every experiment. Initial capability growth, stocks, investment, and
research-compute spending are held fixed. Each accelerating path ends when annual
capital growth reaches 50 percent; the stopping rule avoids treating the reduced
numerical closure as a quantitatively meaningful description arbitrarily close to a
singularity.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_sigma_hm_one_sigma_xl.png}
    \caption{Production-side acceleration when human research remains essential.
    Research is Cobb--Douglas, $\sigma_{HM}=1$, and the automated contribution share is
    fixed at $\omega_M=0.35$. The $\sigma_{XL}>1$ paths end when capital growth reaches 50
    percent per year.}
    \label{fig:numerical-sigma-hm-one-sigma-xl}
\end{figure}

The $\sigma_{XL}=1$ control does not reach the cutoff over 800 years. At the end of the
simulation, capability growth is 1.74 percent, capital growth is 1.64 percent, and
per-capita output growth is 0.44 percent. By contrast, the $\sigma_{XL}=1.1$ path reaches
the cutoff in year 733.5, when capability growth is 11.48 percent and per-capita
output growth is 57.66 percent. With $\sigma_{XL}=2$, the cutoff arrives in year 171.5;
capability growth is then 10.59 percent and per-capita output growth is 66.39
percent. Even modest gross substitution therefore changes the asymptotic regime,
although it may take centuries to become visible.

The experiment also exposes a boundary that a full equilibrium must confront. The
human-research share of population reaches 11.17 percent in the $\sigma_{XL}=1.1$ path
and 52.32 percent when $\sigma_{XL}=2$. Research remains human-essential, and the scale
effect generated by rapid output growth pulls workers into AI development rather
than making them disappear. The numerical paths confirm the absence of balanced
growth under the fixed-share closure; they do not by themselves prove that the fully
optimizing monopoly equilibrium reaches a finite-time singularity. That stronger
claim depends on whether saving and research shares remain positive before the labor
allocation or another physical constraint binds.

\subsubsection{Production complementarity and labor income}

The case $\sigma_{HM}=1$ also delivers a sharper labor-share result. The
Cobb--Douglas research first-order condition implies
\begin{equation}
    w_tH_t
    =
    \frac{\omega_H}{\omega_M}\xi M_t.
    \label{eq:cd-research-wage-bill}
\end{equation}
Combining this condition with the numerical rule
$\xi M_t=m_RY_t$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{w_tH_t}{Y_t}
    =
    \frac{\omega_H}{\omega_M}m_R,
    \qquad
    \ell^N_t
    =
    \ell^Y_t+\frac{\omega_H}{\omega_M}m_R.
    \label{eq:cd-research-aggregate-labor-share}
\end{equation}
The wage bill of human researchers is therefore a constant share of output even
when $H_t/N_t$ changes. Under this closure, the aggregate and production
labor-income shares change by exactly the same number of percentage points. If AI
services grow relative to production labor, Proposition
\ref{prop:wage-share-thresholds} then implies that both shares rise when
$\sigma_{XL}<1$, remain constant when $\sigma_{XL}=1$, and fall when
$\sigma_{XL}>1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-sigma-hm-one-low-sigma-xl} compares
$\sigma_{XL}=0.5$ and $0.75$ with the Cobb--Douglas production control. The initial
stocks, initial capability growth, spending shares, and all other parameters are
common. Each path contains 1,601 half-year observations over 800 years.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_sigma_hm_one_low_sigma_xl.png}
    \caption{Labor-income shares under Cobb--Douglas research and production
    complementarity. The research elasticity is $\sigma_{HM}=1$. The first two
    panels report shares of output. The last two panels show the production
    composition and the allocation of human researchers.}
    \label{fig:numerical-sigma-hm-one-low-sigma-xl}
\end{figure}

With $\sigma_{XL}=0.75$, the production-labor share rises from 36.07 to 44.74
percent and the aggregate labor-income share rises from 37.01 to 45.69 percent.
Both increase by 8.67 percentage points. With $\sigma_{XL}=0.5$, the corresponding
increases are 10.36 percentage points: the production share rises from 16.52 to
26.88 percent and the aggregate share from 17.46 to 27.83 percent. Under
Cobb--Douglas production, the production and aggregate shares remain constant at
53.60 and 54.55 percent.

The mechanism is visible in the lower panels. When $\sigma_{XL}<1$, the AI
expenditure share falls as the service-to-labor ratio expands. Production labor
therefore receives a larger share of output. The human-research employment share
also falls, but equation~\eqref{eq:cd-research-wage-bill} keeps the researchers'
wage bill at 0.95 percent of output. The level comparison across elasticities should
not be read as a causal ranking: holding the CES weights
$(\omega_L,\omega_X)$ fixed changes the
date-zero expenditure share when $\sigma_{XL}$ changes. The robust comparison is
within each path.

The distributional protection comes with a growth trade-off in this
parameterization. In both $\sigma_{XL}<1$ experiments, per-capita output growth is
less than 0.001 percent by year 800 and aggregate capital growth converges to
population growth. Complementarity makes production labor essential, so further
capability improvements have a vanishing effect on output per person.

\subsection{Scope of the numerical evidence}

The experiments establish that the analytical regime distinctions survive in a
closed transition system and that convergence can be much slower than the
asymptotic formulas suggest. They also establish that real-wage declines are
possible, including along a path with positive capital deepening, when production
substitution is sufficiently strong. Finally, they show that human essentiality in
research does not restore balanced growth when $\sigma_{XL}>1$ under the fixed-share
closure. When $\sigma_{HM}=1$, the same closure implies that production and
aggregate labor-income shares rise together for $\sigma_{XL}<1$ and remain constant
at $\sigma_{XL}=1$. They do not yet provide welfare comparisons or
a quantitative prediction about calendar time. The next numerical step is to
replace \eqref{eq:numerical-share-rules} with the household Euler equation, the
developer's costate equation, and transversality conditions. That solution will
determine research and saving endogenously and will be required before comparing
regulatory regimes.
