\section{Numerical transition experiments}
\label{sec:numerical}

This section uses numerical experiments to distinguish transitional dynamics from
asymptotic growth. The exercises are illustrative rather than a quantitative
calibration. They preserve the production CES, monopoly service supply, the
cost-minimizing CES research mix, population growth, capital accumulation, and the
ideas production function. They do not yet solve the full saddle path for household
consumption and the developer's shadow value of capability.

\subsection{Numerical closure and baseline}

To close the transition system transparently, investment and automated-research
spending are fixed at constant shares of output:
\begin{equation}
    I_t=s_IY_t,
    \qquad
    \xi M_t=m_RY_t.
    \label{eq:numerical-share-rules}
\end{equation}
Conditional on $(K_t,A_t,N_t)$, the algorithm jointly solves the monopoly condition
\eqref{eq:monopoly-ai-price}, labor-market clearing, and the research-input ratio
\eqref{eq:research-input-ratio}. The state variables then follow
\begin{align}
    \dot K_t&=s_IY_t-\delta_KK_t,
    &
    \dot A_t&=\chi A_t^\phi E_t^\eta,
    &
    \dot N_t&=nN_t.
    \label{eq:numerical-state-system}
\end{align}
Consumption is the residual
\begin{equation}
    \frac{C_t}{Y_t}
    =
    1-s_I-m_R-\frac{\xi U_t}{Y_t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-consumption}
\end{equation}
The closure in \eqref{eq:numerical-share-rules} isolates the technological and
market-structure feedbacks. It should not be interpreted as the developer's optimal
research policy away from the balanced-growth path.

Table~\ref{tab:numerical-calibration} reports the baseline. The investment and
research-compute shares are obtained from
\eqref{eq:bg-research-resource-share} and
\eqref{eq:bg-investment-share}. In the Cobb--Douglas benchmark they imply an
AI-dominated capability growth rate of 2.27 percent and per-capita growth of 0.57
percent. The scale parameter $\chi$ is normalized so that initial capability growth
equals 2.27 percent. In comparisons that change a central elasticity, $\chi$ is
renormalized to retain the same initial growth rate. This makes the subsequent
divergence a dynamic result rather than a difference imposed at date zero.

The initial stocks are $A_0=N_0=1$ and $K_0=2.509$. The common compute cost is
normalized to $\xi=1$. All reported terminal growth rates are averages over the
last ten simulated years. The static monopoly and research-mix residuals are below
$10^{-6}$, and the consumption share remains positive in every experiment.

\subsection{Production substitution}

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-production-regimes} changes only the production elasticity
$\sigma$ between production labor and AI services, apart from the normalization
of $\chi$ described above. Each output and wage series is expressed as the
base-10 logarithm of its own date-zero index. The figure confirms that $\sigma=1$
is not a neutral approximation to a general CES transition.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_production_regimes.png}
    \caption{Numerical transitions across production elasticities. The allocation
    rules and initial capability growth are common across cases. The
    strong-substitution path ends when capital growth crosses 50 percent per year.}
    \label{fig:numerical-production-regimes}
\end{figure}

With $\sigma=0.75$, per-capita output growth is 0.18 percent in year 160 and
capability growth is 1.54 percent. The AI contribution to the service composite
converges toward an interior value, so the production-labor income share rises from
36.4 to 43.1 percent. Capability continues to grow because the numerical closure
holds research spending positive, but its productive effect becomes progressively
smaller.

With $\sigma=1$, the production-labor income share remains at 53.6 percent. At year
160, capability growth is 1.58 percent and per-capita output growth is 0.42 percent,
both below their AI-dominated limits. The reason is not a different steady state:
automated services still account for only 20.1 percent of effective research. The
transition to the asymptotic regime is slow.

With $\sigma=2$, per-capita output growth reaches 4.10 percent and the real wage
grows by 1.77 percent in year 160, while the production-labor income share falls to
29.4 percent. This is the dynamic counterpart of the interval
$1<\sigma<1/\alpha$: wages and labor's share move in opposite directions. With
$\sigma=4$, the production-labor income share falls to 2.1 percent and ten-year
average per-capita growth reaches 36.1 percent before the path stops in year 135.5,
when capital growth crosses the 50 percent cutoff. These values are not a steady
state; they mark the acceleration regime described in Proposition
\ref{prop:production-ces-regimes}.

\subsection{When does labor's income share rise or fall?}

Proposition~\ref{prop:dynamic-labor-share} separates the income share paid to
production workers from the aggregate labor-income share, which also includes human
researchers. Throughout this subsection, $\sigma$ denotes substitution between
$L_Y$ and $X$ in production; it is distinct from the research elasticity $\theta$
between $H$ and $M$. The production-labor share satisfies
\eqref{eq:dynamic-production-labor-share}. It rises when the substitution term
\[
    \left(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)
    \left(g_X-g_{L_Y}\right)
\]
is negative and falls when it is positive. Thus, if AI services per production
worker increase, the threshold is $\sigma=1$: complementarity raises the share,
Cobb--Douglas production keeps it constant, and gross substitution lowers it. This
condition does not depend directly on capital accumulation.

The aggregate labor-income share adds the reallocation term
$\dot h/(1-h)=n-g_{L_Y}$, where $h=H/N$ is the human-research share of population.
An increase in $h$ raises the aggregate share relative to the production-labor
share; a decrease in $h$ lowers it. The aggregate share therefore rises if
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dot h_t}{1-h_t}
    >
    \left(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)s^X_t
    \left(g_{X,t}-g_{L_Y,t}\right),
    \label{eq:numerical-aggregate-share-rise}
\end{equation}
and falls if the inequality is reversed. When the research-employment share is
constant, the reallocation term is zero and the $\sigma=1$ threshold applies to
both labor-income shares.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_labor_share_paths.png}
    \caption{Production and aggregate labor-income shares across production
    elasticities. The aggregate share includes the wage bill of human researchers.
    The strong-substitution path ends at the acceleration cutoff.}
    \label{fig:numerical-labor-shares}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-labor-shares} shows that the distinction is quantitatively
relevant. With $\sigma=0.75$, the production-labor share rises from 36.3 to 43.2
percent of output. The aggregate share rises from 43.7 to 47.3 percent. Its increase
is smaller because the human-research share of population falls from 17.0 to 8.7
percent. With $\sigma=1$, the production-labor share remains exactly equal to 53.6
percent, but the aggregate share falls from 57.8 to 55.6 percent because the
human-research share falls from 7.3 to 3.6 percent.

With $\sigma=2$, the human-research share changes little and both income shares fall
by approximately 60.5 percent relative to their initial values. With $\sigma=4$,
the production-labor share falls from 67.0 percent to 0.05 percent before the path
reaches the acceleration cutoff. Human research absorbs 90.9 percent of the
population at that point, so the aggregate labor-income share remains above the
production-labor share, but it still falls from 69.3 to 0.55 percent. A large
reallocation into research can attenuate the fall in the aggregate share; in this
experiment it cannot offset the displacement term.

\subsection{When can the real wage fall?}

The static threshold $\sigma=1/\alpha$ is not, by itself, a prediction for the
equilibrium wage path because capital, AI services, and production labor all move.
Define capital and AI-services deepening relative to production labor as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{K,t}\equiv g_{K,t}-g_{L_Y,t},
    \qquad
    \Delta_{X,t}\equiv g_{X,t}-g_{L_Y,t}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:dynamic-wage-decomposition} implies the exact condition
\begin{equation}
    g_{w,t}
    =
    \alpha\Delta_{K,t}
    +
    s^X_t\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}-\alpha\right)\Delta_{X,t}
    <0.
    \label{eq:numerical-wage-decline-condition}
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $\sigma\leq 1/\alpha$ and both forms of deepening are weakly
positive, the wage cannot fall. This is a sufficient condition, not an unconditional
theorem: the wage can still fall if capital per production worker contracts. When
$\sigma>1/\alpha$ and $\Delta_{X,t}>0$, the relevant condition becomes
\begin{equation}
    \alpha\Delta_{K,t}
    <
    s^X_t\left(\alpha-\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)\Delta_{X,t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-wage-decline-threshold}
\end{equation}
Thus high substitutability is not sufficient either. AI services must grow rapidly
relative to production labor, their expenditure share must be quantitatively
important, and capital deepening must be insufficient to offset the displacement
term.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-wage-paths} compares four 80-year transitions. The
reference path uses $n=1.2$ percent, $\sigma=4$, and the baseline investment share
$s_I=23.34$ percent. The second raises population growth to 3 percent. We do not use
larger values because the household problem requires $\rho>n$ and the calibration
sets $\rho=4$ percent. Neither path falls below its initial wage. In the high
population case, the wage is 17.0 percent above its initial level in year 80.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_wage_decline_paths.png}
    \caption{Real-wage transitions under high population growth, strong production
    substitution, and alternative investment shares. Each quantity index is
    normalized to one at date zero.}
    \label{fig:numerical-wage-paths}
\end{figure}

The third path retains $n=3$ percent and the baseline investment share but raises
$\sigma$ to 10. The wage first rises, peaks 12.9 percent above its initial value in
year 50, and then declines. It crosses below its initial level in year 77 and ends
4.3 percent below it, with wage growth equal to $-1.50$ percent. At year 80, capital
deepening contributes 3.80 percentage points to wage growth, while AI-services
deepening contributes $-5.26$ percentage points. Strong substitution therefore
produces a wage decline despite rapid growth of capital per production worker.
Reducing the investment share to 15 percent weakens the offsetting force: the wage
falls from the start and is 18.2 percent below its initial value in year 80.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-wage-frontier} broadens the comparison. Each reported
number is the lowest percentage change relative to the initial wage over 80 years.
A zero therefore means that the wage never crosses below its initial value; it does
not rule out a decline from an intervening peak. At $\sigma=4$ and $n=3$ percent,
an investment share of 15 percent produces a cumulative loss of 9 percent, whereas
shares of 20 percent or more prevent a loss relative to date zero over the simulated
horizon. At $n=3$ percent and $s_I=20$ percent, the wage remains above its initial
level through $\sigma=6$, but falls 4.7 percent below it at $\sigma=8$ and 9.1
percent below it at $\sigma=10$. Even with the baseline investment share, the
$\sigma=10$ case ends 4.3 percent below the initial wage.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_wage_decline_frontier.png}
    \caption{Numerical frontier for cumulative real-wage losses over 80 years.
    The left panel fixes $\sigma=4$; the right panel fixes population growth at
    3 percent.}
    \label{fig:numerical-wage-frontier}
\end{figure}

These experiments answer the qualitative question but not yet the quantitative one.
There is no theorem that wages always rise in the current model. There is instead a
sufficient non-decline condition and an endogenous boundary summarized by
\eqref{eq:numerical-wage-decline-condition}. In this numerical closure, population
growth pushes toward wage decline by increasing labor supply and diluting capital,
while investment pushes in the opposite direction. Substitution changes the sign
and magnitude of the AI-services term. Because $s_I$ is fixed in these experiments,
the exact numerical boundary is provisional. An optimizing household may respond to
the prospect of lower wages and higher capital returns by changing saving, which
will shift the frontier.

\subsection{The speed of research automation}

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-research-automation} changes the research elasticity
$\theta$ between $H$ and $M$ while holding the
initial automated-research share at 10.9 percent and initial capability growth at
2.27 percent. The CES weight $\nu$ and the scale parameter $\chi$ are renormalized
for this comparison. The exercise therefore isolates the curvature of substitution
rather than confounding it with a different initial input mix.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_research_automation.png}
    \caption{Research-automation transitions under Cobb--Douglas production.
    The initial automated-research share and capability growth rate are common
    across cases.}
    \label{fig:numerical-research-automation}
\end{figure}

After 400 years, automated services account for 16.0 percent of effective research
when $\theta=1.25$, 38.3 percent when $\theta=2$, and 90.4 percent when
$\theta=4$. The corresponding human-research shares of population are 4.77, 1.51,
and 0.10 percent. A larger elasticity therefore changes the input composition much
more quickly. It does not imply an immediate increase in capability growth.
Capability growth initially falls in all three cases and, with $\theta=4$, begins
to recover only after automated research has become quantitatively important. The
automation share is thus a state of the transition, not a sufficient statistic for
the growth rate.

\subsection{Automation intensity and the scale of AI research}

The decline of human research relative to automated research does not determine
whether the development industry employs more or fewer people. Equation
\eqref{eq:human-research-scale-identity} gives the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}
    \frac{H_t}{N_t}
    =
    \frac{H_t}{M_t}\frac{M_t}{N_t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-research-employment-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first ratio measures the human intensity of research. The second measures the
scale of automated research relative to the population. Under the numerical rule
$\xi M_t=m_RY_t$ and the normalization $g_\xi=0$, their growth rates imply
\begin{equation}
    g_{H/N,t}
    =
    g_{Y,t}-n-\theta g_{w,t}.
    \label{eq:numerical-research-employment-growth}
\end{equation}
Human research employment therefore rises when the expansion of output per capita,
and hence of automated-research spending per capita, exceeds the reduction in human
intensity induced by wage growth.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-research-labor-dynamics} compares $\sigma=1$, $2$, and
$2.25$, holding $\theta=2$, the initial stocks, the spending rules, and initial
capability growth fixed. The cases begin with different research-input ratios because
the production elasticity changes the date-zero wage. This is an equilibrium level
effect rather than a different initial state.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_research_labor_dynamics.png}
    \caption{Human employment during AI research automation. The first two panels
    report the human-to-automated research ratio and automated research per capita on
    logarithmic scales. The third reports human researchers as a percentage of the
    population, and the fourth reports the growth rate of that share. The
    $\sigma=2.25$ path ends when capital growth crosses 50 percent per year.}
    \label{fig:numerical-research-labor-dynamics}
\end{figure}

With $\sigma=1$, $H/M$ falls from 19.51 to 3.78 over 180 years and $H/N$ falls
monotonically from 7.25 to 3.33 percent. With $\sigma=2$, $H/M$ falls more sharply,
from 7.32 to 0.29. Nevertheless, $H/N$ reaches a minimum of approximately 3.20
percent around year 65 and then rises to 4.77 percent in year 180. With
$\sigma=2.25$, $H/M$ falls from 6.96 to 0.018, while $H/N$ falls initially from
3.65 to approximately 3.11 percent and then rises to 19.46 percent before the path
reaches the acceleration cutoff in year 178.

The last path is not a prediction that one fifth of the population will work in AI
research. It identifies a general-equilibrium mechanism. Rapid output growth expands
$M/N$ from 0.0052 to 10.84, which more than offsets the decline in human researchers
required per unit of automated research. The development industry becomes less human
intensive and employs a larger fraction of the population at the same time. Because
the numerical closure fixes $m_R$, an optimizing developer may move the turning point
or eliminate it. The experiment establishes that $H/M\to0$ is not sufficient for
$H/N\to0$.

\subsection{Initial conditions and asymptotic growth}

Steady-state language requires care. Parameters determine a unique stable
balanced-growth path in the baseline numerical system. Initial conditions determine
the transition toward that path; they determine the asymptotic outcome only if the
model has multiple attractors, thresholds, or an explosive region.

Figure~\ref{fig:numerical-initial-conditions} holds all parameters fixed and sets
$A_0$ equal to 0.5, 1, or 2. Initial capital and population are common. The output
series use the output level in the $A_0=1$ economy at date zero as a common index,
so convergence in the figure is convergence in levels rather than an artifact of
separate normalization.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/numerical_initial_conditions.png}
    \caption{Transitions from different initial capability stocks in the stable
    Cobb--Douglas parameterization.}
    \label{fig:numerical-initial-conditions}
\end{figure}

The initial capability stock has a large effect on early capability growth: it is
3.0 percent when $A_0=0.5$ and 1.7 percent when $A_0=2$. By year 800, however, the
three capability growth rates lie between 1.64 and 1.65 percent, and the output
levels differ by less than 0.4 percent. Initial conditions mainly shift the
transition. The common growth rate is still below the analytical limit of 2.27
percent because automated services account for only about 73.6 percent of effective
research even after eight centuries. This slow convergence makes it useful to call
the observed finite-horizon path a \emph{transitional growth regime}, not a
``semi-steady state.''

\subsection{Research feedback and acceleration}

The final experiment holds $\sigma=1$ and compares the stable baseline,
$1-\phi-\eta\upsilon=0.2375$, with a high-feedback case
$(\phi,\eta)=(0.86,0.62)$, for which
$1-\phi-\eta\upsilon=-0.015$. Initial capability growth and the allocation shares
are again common.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/numerical_growth_feedback.png}
    \caption{Stable and accelerating research-feedback regimes under
    Cobb--Douglas production.}
    \label{fig:numerical-growth-feedback}
\end{figure}

In the stable case, capability growth is 1.50 percent in year 600 and automated
services account for 56.2 percent of effective research. In the high-feedback case,
the automated share is 99.6 percent, capability growth reaches 18.3 percent, and
per-capita output growth reaches 4.32 percent. The latter path does not approach a
positive constant growth rate. It illustrates why failure of
\eqref{eq:cd-ai-bg-condition} should be interpreted as a candidate acceleration
regime rather than as another balanced-growth path.

\subsection{Scope of the numerical evidence}

The experiments establish that the analytical regime distinctions survive in a
closed transition system and that convergence can be much slower than the
asymptotic formulas suggest. They also establish that real-wage declines are
possible, including along a path with positive capital deepening, when production
substitution is sufficiently strong. They do not yet provide welfare comparisons or
a quantitative prediction about calendar time. The next numerical step is to
replace \eqref{eq:numerical-share-rules} with the household Euler equation, the
developer's costate equation, and transversality conditions. That solution will
determine research and saving endogenously and will be required before comparing
regulatory regimes.
